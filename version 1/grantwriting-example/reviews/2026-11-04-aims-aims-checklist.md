## 60-second reviewer test

Answered from the page alone.

1. **What is the problem?** Genome-scale data offer too many combinations of variables to search by brute force, so genomic predictors of disease, here breast cancer progression, remain rare in practice. The page states this only from its fourth sentence, and says little about why it matters.
2. **What don't we know?** Whether crowdsourcing through games, already used for protein structure, sequence alignment and RNA design, can be extended to building predictive models from genome-scale data.
3. **What is the hypothesis?** That serious games can attract many biologists whose in-game actions supply the insight needed to build state-of-the-art predictors of disease progression. The page gives no threshold or comparator, so I cannot say what result would count against it.
4. **What will be done?** A game will be built (Aim 1), and a framework that weights players, aggregates the community's assertions and combines them with experimental data to infer predictors (Aim 2). Beyond that the page doesn't let me answer: it does not say what players do in the game, which data or datasets are used, or how a predictor will be judged.
5. **What do we gain if it works?** Better predictors of breast cancer progression, plus a reusable, open-source framework for capturing community knowledge in biology. The page does not say better than what, or by how much.

## The five most important problems, most serious first

1. **The page does not say what will be done.** Each aim is a title followed by one hypothesis sentence. No sentence gives the overall approach, since the line before the aims only announces them. The aims take 127 of the page's 669 words. A reviewer can name the two aims but cannot say what players do, what data result, which breast cancer datasets are used, or how success is measured. PAR-09-219 says R21 reviewers "will place less emphasis on methodological details", so the gap here is not missing method detail. It is that the work itself is never described.
2. **The central hypothesis cannot fail as written, and its rationale is missing.** "serious games can be developed that attract large numbers of biologist players" sets no threshold. "state of the art predictors" gives no comparator. Aim 1's working hypothesis is confirmed by building and launching the game. The sentence labeled as the rationale describes what the project will deliver, not why the hypothesis should hold. The announcement says reviewers will focus on "the conceptual framework", and that is where this page is weakest.
3. **Aim 2 depends on Aim 1, and the page offers no fallback.** Aim 2 integrates "data from games", which only Aim 1 produces. The Approach says so itself: "If no one ends up playing this game, the value from this project will be minimal". It also describes Aim 2 simulations run in parallel with Aim 1. The page mentions neither, so a reader of the page alone sees a project resting on a single risk.
4. **The opening leads with chess rather than the problem, and ends without a need.** Sentences 1 to 3 are about games and the authors' plan. The problem arrives in sentence 4. The paragraph stops at the gap, with no statement of the need it creates. A reviewer's first impression is the method rather than the problem it serves, and the closing paragraph cannot loop back to a first sentence about chess.
5. **The claims run ahead of a two-year exploratory award, and the evidence the page leans on is not shown.** Five phrases promise more than an exploratory test: "highly accurate", "state of the art", "a fundamentally new strategy", "supporting new predictive tests for breast cancer patients" and "validated design patterns". The proof-of-concept is the page's only support for the hypothesis, and the page gives none of its numbers. The Approach reports more than 120 players in a week and 65% against 55% cross-validated accuracy. The page's claim that the game gathered "useful data, not present in public databases" is not supported in the Approach's Preliminary Data or in `evidence/`.

## What I would have asked the author

The author was not available, so the review proceeded on what the folder holds. These are the questions I would have asked:

- Is there a course or department Aims checklist that has not been put in `checklists/`? The built-in checklist was used instead.
- What font, size, margins and page size did the submitted Aims page use, or is there a PDF of it? Without one, whether the page fits on one page is unknown.
- Which application instructions governed the October 2012 Specific Aims page limit? `funder/` does not hold them.
- What do "state of the art" and "better" compare against: which predictors, on which datasets?
- What are the "useful data, not present in public databases" that the proof-of-concept gathered?
- The README names Andrew Su as PI and Benjamin Good as Co-Investigator. Is this jointly written page the author's own for the purpose of the folder's rule on reviewing only the author's drafts? This review assumed it is.
