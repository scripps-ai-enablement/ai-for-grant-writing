# Biosketch draft: Benjamin M. Good

## Personal Statement

My training spans cognitive science (B.S.), evolutionary and adaptive systems (M.S.) and bioinformatics (Ph.D.), and my research has focused on acquiring and validating structured knowledge from large groups of biologists through web-based community intelligence applications.

As a graduate student I developed a game-like system for ontology engineering, and I studied ontology development by volunteers without expert curation (Products 3, 8). I have since co-authored an article on games with a scientific purpose (Product 2), and I designed and implemented The Cure, a web-based game for building predictors of breast cancer survival from gene expression and copy number data, released in September 2012 (Product 1). This work directly informs Aim 1, in which we will implement a serious game that attracts biologists with relevant expertise and captures their insight through gameplay.

I have also investigated open, Web-based document classification, characterizing social tagging as a metadata resource for bioinformatics (Product 4). Since my postdoctoral fellowship I have led efforts to extract ontology-based gene annotations from the text of the Gene Wiki (Product 5) and to motivate the community to contribute such annotations directly (Products 6, 7). This work directly informs Aim 2, in which we will weight game data by each participant's ability and aggregate it by voting into confidence estimates for the community's assertions.

I trained in machine learning during my M.S. and as a research associate at UC San Diego, and before my Ph.D. I led bioinformatics projects ranging from a genome database for the pathogenic fungus Coccidioides immitis to sequence-based classifiers predicting HIV co-receptor usage (Product 9). This experience informs the second half of Aim 2, in which we will use those assertions to improve genomic predictors of breast cancer progression inferred from experimental measurements.

My expertise also includes human computer interaction design, the semantic web and biomedical ontologies, and I have professional experience developing interactive Web and client-server software. In the Gene Wiki work this has meant building a semantic network in Wikipedia (Product 7) and linking genes to diseases with a SNPedia-Gene Wiki mashup (Product 10). It prepares me to build the open source software and validated design patterns this project will release so that others can apply the method in other biological contexts.

Early career work developing knowledge-based intelligent systems, as a knowledge engineer and an expert system consultant, gave me a foundation in knowledge-based computing, and in 2009 I co-founded Freeform Information. I have published 36 peer-reviewed articles, and since 2011 I have been a Research Associate at The Scripps Research Institute.

## Contribution: Games and volunteer systems for capturing biological knowledge

Building structured biological knowledge such as an ontology has been expensive and labor-intensive under most existing methods. Crowdsourcing offers another route, and games have been used to bring human knowledge and reasoning to protein structure determination, multiple sequence alignment and RNA structure design. My work in this area asks whether systems of this kind can recruit people with biological knowledge and capture what they know.

As a graduate student I developed a game-like system for ontology engineering. I also tested a zero curation model for ontology development: during two days at a conference on circulatory and respiratory health, 68 volunteers untrained in knowledge engineering used a targeted, web-based interface to create a shared vocabulary of 661 terms, linking the terms to each other and to an existing upper ontology with 245 hyponym and 340 synonym relationships. The rudimentary ontology cost three t-shirts, four coffee mugs and one chocolate moose, and we assessed it quantitatively and qualitatively (Product 3). A second paper addressed ontology engineering using volunteer labor (Product 8).

With Andrew Su I later wrote on games with a scientific purpose, drawing on the protein folding game Foldit, which shows that games are an effective way to recruit, engage and organize ordinary citizens to help solve difficult scientific problems (Product 2). I then designed and implemented The Cure, a web-based game released in September 2012 for building predictors of breast cancer survival from gene expression and copy number data (Product 1).

I was first author of each of these papers, and I designed and implemented The Cure. Together they are the basis for Aim 1, which will implement a serious game for building predictors of breast cancer progression and test whether biologists' insight can be captured through gameplay.

## Contribution: Measuring and using community-generated annotation

Ontology-based gene annotations are important tools for organizing and analyzing genome-scale data, but collecting them is costly. Open, community-built resources such as social tagging systems and Wikipedia can produce annotation at low cost, but its quality and uses must be established. My work measures, aggregates and structures such contributions.

As a graduate student I characterized the metadata that users of two social tagging systems, CiteULike and Connotea, attached to citations in PubMed, measuring document coverage, tag density, inter-annotator agreement and agreement with MeSH indexing. Agreement was low, but agreement between the aggregated tags and MeSH could be increased through voting (Product 4).

Since my postdoctoral fellowship I have led efforts to extract ontology-based gene annotations from the Gene Wiki, Wikipedia's articles on more than 10,000 human genes, and to motivate the community to contribute such annotations directly. From Gene Wiki text we produced 2,983 candidate Disease Ontology and 11,022 candidate Gene Ontology annotations, with estimated precision of 90-93% and 48-64%, and showed they can improve gene set enrichment analyses (Product 5). We described improvements to the system that keeps structured data on Gene Wiki articles in sync with primary databases, and the same paper introduced WikiTrust, which computes the quality of Wikipedia articles at the word level (Product 6). We introduced a method that lets Wikipedia editors embed computable semantic relations in articles (Product 7), and built the Gene Wiki+, which integrates the Gene Wiki with SNPedia and captures 8,047 distinct gene-disease relationships, only 479 of them found independently in both (Product 10).

I was first author of each of these papers. This work informs Aim 2, which will weight game data by participant ability, aggregate it by voting into confidence estimates, and use it with experimental measurements to infer predictors of breast cancer progression.
