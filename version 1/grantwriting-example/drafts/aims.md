# Specific Aims

As any chess player knows, games are powerful motivators for intense, extended human effort. 'Serious games' make use of such effort among large player communities to accomplish real-world objectives. We aim to deploy a serious game to support the development of accurate genomic predictors of the progression of breast cancer. It has long been appreciated that predictive models based on genomic data could be powerful tools in the clinic and in the laboratory yet, in practice, they remain rare. This is, in part, because the combinatorial space of independent variables generated by genome-scale methods is so large. Brute force computational approaches to identifying the specific variable subsets that produce the best integrated signals are unfeasible because of both the size of the space and problems of multiple-testing. Knowledge-based techniques are useful for targeting this search but are limited by the availability and specificity of structured knowledge. Crowdsourcing through games has recently been used successfully to rapidly inject large-scale human knowledge and reasoning ('human computation') into frameworks for solving biological problems involving massive search spaces including: protein structure determination, multiple sequence alignment, and RNA structure design. What is not known is whether the emerging paradigm of scientific crowdsourcing through games can be extended to help address the challenge of translating genome-scale data into useful predictive models.

Our long-term goal is to develop a complete system for inferring highly accurate, predictive computational models of complex diseases that can be used to guide treatment and direct biological research. Such a system will involve massive computational power that is guided and shaped by human insight. The overall objective of this application is to ascertain how the diverse human insight needed to achieve the long-term goal can be rapidly and effectively captured and integrated. The central hypothesis is that serious games can be developed that attract large numbers of biologist players that, through the actions they take in these games, contribute the collective insight needed to build state of the art predictors of disease progression. This working hypothesis was formulated based on the results of a proof-of-concept experiment that showed that it is possible to rapidly recruit many biologists to play scientific games and that useful data, not present in public databases, can be gathered through their play (see Preliminary Data). The rationale that underlies the proposed research is that the output of this project will make it possible to rapidly capture diverse biological knowledge that will improve the inference of predictive models of breast cancer progression. The resultant open source software and validated design patterns will also allow other researchers to apply this new methodology within other biological contexts. We plan to test our central hypothesis and, thereby, accomplish the objective of this application by pursuing the following two specific aims:

**Specific Aim #1: Implement a serious game for building predictors of breast cancer progression.** The working hypothesis is that enjoyable games can be created that attract play from large numbers of biologists with relevant expertise and that their biological insights can be captured through gameplay.

**Specific Aim #2: Create a computational framework that integrates data from games with data from biological experiments to infer genomic predictors of breast cancer progression.** We hypothesize that factors pertaining to participant's abilities can be detected and used to appropriately weight data quality, that aggregation strategies such as voting can be used to compute accurate confidence estimates in assertions made by the community and that these assertions can be used to improve predictive models inferred directly from experimental measurements of biological samples.

With respect to expected outcomes, the work proposed in aims 1 and 2 is expected to generate better predictors of disease progression in breast cancer as well as a re-usable framework for capturing and applying community intelligence in biology. Such results are expected to have an important positive impact, by supporting new predictive tests for breast cancer patients and by providing the biomedical computing community with a fundamentally new strategy for capturing and applying the knowledge needed to translate genomic data into useful predictive models.
