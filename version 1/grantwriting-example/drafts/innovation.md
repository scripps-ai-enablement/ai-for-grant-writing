# Research Strategy: Innovation

**Innovation.** While many different approaches exist, the standard paradigm thus far for translating high throughput (e.g. whole genome RNA expression profiles) experimental data into predictors of disease progression follows this basic pattern: (1) assemble a discovery/training dataset, (2) rank attributes according to some univariate statistic, (3) select the top N arbitrarily, (4) select a classification algorithm, (5) evaluate performance in cross-validation experiments and on external test datasets. The predictors generated with this approach consistently have problems in secondary and tertiary validation studies and in the stability of the genes selected using different training datasets [4]. Recently, methods driven by structured prior knowledge in the form of protein-protein interaction networks [5, 6] and pathway databases [7, 8] have been introduced. These methods guide the search for predictive gene sets towards groups that are biologically meaningful. In doing so, they have made minor improvements in predictive accuracy but yield far more consistent results in terms of the genes that are identified for the same phenotype but different datasets. These knowledge-based techniques hint at the potential of strategies that marry a top-down approach based on established wisdom and human insight with a bottom-up approach based directly on experimental data, but they have not yet produced substantially greater accuracy than other approaches. We contend that this is due in part to the lack of relevant structured knowledge to compute with. The proposed research seeks to provide a mechanism to rapidly, inexpensively and directly capture targeted biological knowledge from domain experts that can be used immediately to improve the inference of genomic predictors. The proposed research is innovative, in our opinion, because it alters the process of assembling genomic predictors informed by community knowledge from a passive one that is entirely dependent on the growth of external, general purpose databases to an active one that offers the potential to rapidly generate focused knowledge bases oriented specifically around solving the problem at hand.

The novel approach described here will allow our small research group to generate knowledge-driven predictors of breast cancer metastasis informed by literally hundreds or thousands of biologists. Such a breadth of expertise would be unreachable for almost any laboratory using current approaches. Furthermore, should such an army of minds somehow be raised there are no established techniques for processing the diverse and noisy knowledge that they could offer. The predictors inferred using this approach will be fundamentally different from those produced through other means because they will represent the distributed biological insights of an unprecedented number of biologists. While previous studies from major institutions might legitimately be able to draw on the expertise of upwards of 10 scientists, we will be able to elegantly tap into a hive mind that is one or even two orders of magnitude larger. The diversity of prior knowledge and intuition captured by expanding the pool of contributing scientists will provide predictors that are more effective than could be achieved by prior methods.
