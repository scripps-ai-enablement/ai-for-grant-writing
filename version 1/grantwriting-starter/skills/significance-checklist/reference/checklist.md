# Significance and Innovation checklist (built-in)

## Scientific premise

- The background tells a coherent story of where the field stands, citing what is directly relevant rather than everything.
- The rigor of the key prior studies is addressed: how strong is the evidence the proposal builds on?
- A figure is planned that summarizes current thinking and shows where the problem sits.
- It ends on the specific need or problem this project addresses.

## Significance

- The gap is a specific unknown, and the text says why it blocks progress.
- There is evidence that the need is real, and an explanation of why it exists.
- The significance is stated plainly enough that a reviewer can find it quickly.
- It says who benefits and what changes in the field if the project succeeds.
- It ties the work to the funder's mission.

## Innovation

- What is new is stated in the first paragraph: a new concept, method, target or tool.
- It is contrasted with what exists, and the text says why it is better.
- It builds on established principles and treats prior work with respect.
- It is tied to the aims and to the gap named in Significance.
- It fits the funding opportunity.

## Pitfalls to flag

- **The disease, not the gap**: selling how bad the disease is instead of naming what is unknown.
- **The literature dump**: citing everything, so the gap gets lost.
- **Overreach**: an advance claimed out of proportion to the mechanism (a cure from a small exploratory grant).
- **Unearned "novel" or "first"**: novelty claimed without a contrast to what exists.
- **Buried innovation**: what is new appears late, or only in the Approach.
- **Novelty for its own sake**: a new technique with no reason it matters to the question.
